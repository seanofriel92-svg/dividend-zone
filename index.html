The two halves of an income portfolio

Stocks for income that can grow. Bonds for income you can schedule. Most income investors hold both — and the mix is the whole game.

Half one · Equities

Dividend stocks & blue chips

A dividend is a slice of profit a company mails you — usually every quarter — just for holding the stock. Blue chips are the big, established companies with the cash flow to keep paying through recessions. The gold standard:

  • Dividend Aristocrats — S&P 500 companies with 25+ straight years of dividend increases. 69 companies qualified in March 2026 (S&P Dow Jones Indices), averaging ~2.1% yield — while the full S&P 500 yielded only ~1.1% in early Nov 2026, near multi-decade lows.
  • Dividend Kings — the elite tier: 50+ years of increases, no index membership required (~50–59 companies, depending on the tracker). Procter & Gamble, Genuine Parts and Dover each sit at 69 years; Coca-Cola is at 62.
  • Payout ratio — the share of earnings paid out. Under ~60% is generally comfortable; over 100% means paying out more than the company earns, which can't last.

Half two · Government bonds

Treasuries, TIPS & I Bonds

When you buy a Treasury, you're lending to the U.S. government — and it's considered among the safest IOUs in the world. Interest is exempt from state and local tax. The family:

  • Bills (4–52 weeks), Notes (2–10 years), Bonds (20–30 years) — sold at auction, tradeable any time.
  • TIPS — principal adjusts with inflation; the 10-year TIPS "real yield" was 2.63% on Nov 6, 2026.
  • Series I Bonds — savings bonds paying 5.51% for bonds issued Nov 2026–Apr 2027 (1.40% fixed + inflation), up to $10,000 per person per year at TreasuryDirect.

The ratings, decoded

Stars measure price, not quality

  • Fund stars (1–5) — backward-looking: risk-adjusted past returns vs peers, needs 36+ months of history.
  • Stock stars (1–5) — forward-looking: price vs an analyst's discounted-cash-flow fair-value estimate, adjusted for an Uncertainty rating (Low→Extreme).
  • Moat — Wide = excess returns expected 20+ years; Narrow = 10+ years. Five sources: intangible assets, switching costs, network effect, cost advantage, efficient scale.
  • Medalist (Gold→Negative) — a fund's odds of beating its benchmark over a full cycle, after fees.

How to read a yield without getting burned

  • Yield = annual dividend ÷ price. A yield can spike because the dividend rose — or because the price collapsed. Always ask which.
  • The ex-dividend date is the cutoff: buy before it, you get the next payment; buy on or after it, the seller does.
  • Qualified dividends (most blue-chip U.S. stocks held 60+ days) are taxed at the lower capital-gains rates in a taxable account; bond interest and REIT dividends are usually taxed as ordinary income.
  • DRIP — dividend reinvestment — buys more shares automatically. It's how a 3% yield quietly compounds into real wealth over 20 years.

⚠️ The yield trap

A stock yielding 9–10% is rarely a gift — it's usually the market pricing in a cut. Screens flag three danger signs: payout ratio climbing past 100%, heavy debt with big interest bills (interest gets paid before shareholders do), and a dividend that has stopped growing. When in doubt, the streak and the cash flow statement outrank the headline yield.

The Government Bond Desk

Everything here is a loan to the U.S. Treasury — the borrower behind the world's benchmark interest rate. Bond math in one line: when yields rise, existing bond prices fall, and the longer the maturity, the harder they fall. Hold to maturity, though, and you get every coupon and your principal back on schedule.

4–52 weeks

Treasury Bills

Sold at a discount, paid back at face value — no coupons, simplest thing the Treasury sells. The 3-month yielded 4.19% on Nov 6, 2026. The default home for cash you need within a year.

2–10 years

Notes & the 10-Year

Coupon every six months. The 10-year note (4.86%) is the benchmark rate — mortgages, car loans and stock valuations all price off it.

20–30 years

Treasury Bonds

The long end: 5.26% at 30 years. Highest yield, biggest price swings if you sell early. Best matched to money you truly won't need for decades.

Inflation-linked

TIPS

Principal rises with the CPI, so your return is "real." 10-year TIPS yielded 2.63% above inflation on Nov 6, 2026. Tax wrinkle: you owe tax each year on the inflation adjustment you haven't received in cash yet.

Inflation-linked

Series I Bonds

5.51% composite for bonds issued Nov 1, 2026–Apr 30, 2027 (1.40% fixed for life + inflation reset every 6 months). $10,000/person/year, TreasuryDirect only, locked 12 months, 3-month interest penalty if cashed before 5 years. State-tax free; federal tax can be deferred until you cash out.

Savings bond

Series EE Bonds

Fixed 2.90% for new issues (Nov 2026–Apr 2027) — plus Treasury's standing promise that an EE bond held 20 years is worth twice its purchase price. A slow, certain savings tool, not an income engine.

2 years · floating

FRNs & STRIPS

Floating Rate Notes reset weekly to the 13-week bill rate plus a spread — income that moves with the Fed instead of fighting it. STRIPS split a note or bond into zero-coupon pieces (principal and each coupon sold separately); they're created and sold only through brokers/dealers, never at TreasuryDirect.

Why rates are where they are

The Fed sets one rate — the federal funds target, the overnight rate banks charge each other — and the whole curve leans on it. The FOMC raised the target by 25bp to 3.75–4.00% on September 17, 2026, and held it there on October 28, 2026, by a 10–2 vote — dissenters Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack both wanted another +25bp hike. The effective rate sat inside the range at 3.88% (Nov 5, 2026). The next decision lands December 9, 2026, with a Summary of Economic Projections. What markets expect for December is an open question — estimates in the research ranged from ~65% to ~78.6% for a hike depending on source and date; check CME FedWatch live rather than trusting any single figure. Around the edges: the bank prime rate was 7.0% and the average 30-year fixed mortgage ~7.24% (Freddie Mac, week of Oct 29, 2026). Short yields (bills) track the Fed closely; long yields (the 10- and 30-year) track where investors think the Fed and inflation are heading over years — which is why the 10-year spiked to 5.31% in early October 2026 before settling at 4.86%.

Buying at TreasuryDirect, step by step

Verified live from TreasuryDirect's marketable-securities FAQ (Nov 7, 2026):

  • Open an account with your SSN, a U.S. address and a U.S. bank account — no fees, no minimum balance. (A 2026 move to ID.me sign-in was noted in the research; check the current login steps before following any how-to.)
  • Marketable securities: $100 minimum, in $100 multiples. Savings bonds: $25 minimum. Non-competitive bids up to $10m per auction.
  • BuyDirect tab → security type → amount → auction date. Everyone in an auction gets the same price.
  • New purchases generally stay in TreasuryDirect 45 calendar days before they can transfer to a broker — the only way to sell before maturity. At maturity, principal lands in your bank or can be scheduled to reinvest.
  • Savings-bond cap: $10,000 electronic I Bonds + $10,000 electronic EE Bonds per person per calendar year. Paper I Bonds via tax refund ended Jan 1, 2025.

Municipal bonds, with real numbers

Issued by states and cities, not Washington. Interest is generally exempt from federal tax (and state tax if issued in your state), so compare on a tax-equivalent yield: muni yield ÷ (1 − your federal rate). National muni funds on Nov 5, 2026: VTEB 4.11% (0.03% expense, 7.3-yr duration) and MUB 3.76% (0.05%, 7.13 yrs), 30-day SEC yields. At a 24–37% bracket, VTEB's 4.11% tax-free ≈ 5.4–6.5% taxable — competitive with the 10-year Treasury's 4.86% for high earners, much less so in a low bracket. The trade: munis carry real credit risk (Detroit and Puerto Rico defaulted) and thinner liquidity; Treasuries carry effectively none. There is no free official daily muni index, so fund SEC yields are the labelled stand-in. For context the same week: investment-grade corporates yielded 5.71% and high-yield 8.07% (ICE BofA, Nov 5, 2026) — more pay, more risk, fully taxable.

Early retirement, honestly

The bridges across the 59½ wall

  • Roth conversion ladder — convert traditional IRA money to Roth a little each year in low-income early-retirement years; each conversion becomes withdrawable, penalty-free, after 5 years.
  • Rule 72(t) / SEPP — a rigid IRS schedule of substantially equal payments that unlocks an IRA early without the 10% penalty. Powerful, unforgiving of mistakes.
  • Rule of 55 — leave a job at 55+ and that employer's 401(k) can often be tapped penalty-free (employer plans only — one reason not to roll everything into an IRA too fast).
  • The taxable bridge — simplest of all: spend brokerage money first while the tax-advantaged accounts keep compounding.

What the brochures skip

  • Healthcare is the hard part. Before Medicare at 65, early retirees budget for marketplace (ACA) coverage — and your reported income steers your premium subsidy, which makes Roth vs. traditional a yearly calculation.
  • The "4% rule" is a rule of thumb, from 1990s research (Bengen 1994; the Trinity study), not a law of physics. It survived 30 years in ~94% of historical U.S. periods — but only ~80% over 50 years, the horizon an early retiree actually faces; 3.5% lasted 50 years in ~98%. A bond-only portfolio managed 30 years at 4% in just 39% of periods, and a 1% annual fee cut 30-year success from ~94% to ~87%. Dividend-and-Treasury income covering core expenses is one conservative alternative: spend the paycheck, leave the principal alone.
  • RMDs still arrive. Traditional, SEP, SIMPLE and pre-tax 401(k) money must start coming out at age 73 (born 1951–1959) or age 75 (born 1960+, from 2033). Missing one costs a 25% excise tax on the shortfall (10% if corrected within 2 years). Roth IRAs have no lifetime RMDs at all — one more reason the bridge years matter.
  • Deadlines differ by account. A SEP IRA can be opened and funded as late as your tax filing deadline including extensions (Oct 15, 2027 for 2026); a Solo 401(k) generally must exist by Dec 31 for that year's employee deferrals — though the research flags the exact post-year-end rule for sole proprietors as an open question to confirm in IRS Publication 560.
  • Sequence risk — a crash in your first five retired years does far more damage than the same crash in year twenty. A 2–3 year Treasury ladder of living expenses is the classic shock absorber.
  • Social Security timing still matters: claiming at 62 vs. 70 changes the check by roughly 70%+ for life.

📌 Start-here order most self-directed savers use

HSA if you qualify → Roth IRA → SEP or Solo 401(k) for self-employment income → taxable brokerage for everything above the caps. It's a framework for learning, not a personal recommendation — your tax picture decides the real order, and a one-hour consult with a fiduciary CPA or CFP® can pay for itself here.

Learn: The Income Investor's Field Guide

Short, plain-English answers to the questions every page on this site assumes you know.

What exactly is a dividend — and where does the money come from?

A dividend is a distribution of a company's profits to its shareholders, set by its board of directors, typically paid quarterly in cash. It comes out of real earnings and cash flow — which is why the payout ratio (dividends ÷ earnings) is the first safety check. A company earning $6 a share and paying $4 has a 67% payout: believable. One paying $4 on $3 of earnings is borrowing from its future.

What makes a stock "blue chip"?

No official definition — it's shorthand for large, long-established, financially sound companies with household-name products and long records of paying (and usually raising) dividends: think Procter & Gamble, Johnson & Johnson, Coca-Cola, PepsiCo. Blue chips are the natural hunting ground for income investors because dividend reliability, not growth fireworks, is the point.

Why do bond prices fall when interest rates rise?

Imagine you own a bond paying 3%. New bonds start paying 5%. Nobody will pay full price for your 3% bond — its price drops until its effective yield matches the market. The longer until your bond matures, the bigger the swing (that's duration). If you hold to maturity, the swings are just scenery: you still get every payment and your principal back.

What's the yield curve, and why did everyone fear it "inverting"?

Plot what the Treasury pays at every maturity and you get the yield curve. Normally it slopes up — longer loans, higher rates. When short rates rise above long rates (an inversion), it has historically signaled recession risk, and it ran inverted for much of 2022–2024. As of Nov 6, 2026 it slopes upward again: 3-month 4.19% → 10-year 4.86% → 30-year 5.26%.

How does Morningstar actually rate things? (And what does Dividend Zone do differently?)

Morningstar, founded in Chicago in 1984, became the default language of fund and stock research. Its fund star rating (1–5) is backward-looking: risk-adjusted returns versus peers, recalculated monthly. Its stock star rating is forward-looking: price versus an analyst's discounted-cash-flow fair-value estimate, adjusted for uncertainty. The economic moat rating judges whether a company's competitive edge can protect profits for 10 (narrow) or 20 (wide) years, and its Medalist ratings (Gold→Negative) judge whether a fund can beat its benchmark going forward. Dividend Zone borrows the discipline — fair value, moats, peer comparison — but points every score at income durability instead of total return. Morningstar®, Star Rating™ and Economic Moat™ are Morningstar's marks; this concept is not affiliated with or endorsed by Morningstar, Inc.

Treasury, TIPS, or I Bond — which one for which job?
  • Cash within a year: T-bills (or a Treasury money-market fund).
  • Scheduled income, known dates: notes/bonds in a ladder.
  • Inflation protection you'll hold for years: I Bonds (simple, but capped at $10k/yr and locked a year) or TIPS (no cap, tradeable, trickier taxes).
  • Beating inflation by a set margin: TIPS — the 10-year's 2.63% "real" yield on Nov 6, 2026 is the market's cleanest read on that.
How are dividends and bond interest taxed?

In a taxable U.S. account: qualified dividends (most blue chips, held long enough) get the lower 0%/15%/20% capital-gains rates; Treasury interest is federally taxed but exempt from state and local tax; municipal bond interest is usually federally exempt; I Bond federal tax can be deferred until redemption. Inside IRAs, none of this matters year to year — traditional accounts are taxed at withdrawal, Roth accounts not at all if qualified. General education only; your situation needs a tax professional.

Features that could cross the line, and which this concept therefore avoids or keeps decision-support only: tools that output "buy X", "you should" language tied to a named security for a named person, personalised portfolio reviews, one-to-one guidance by email, and irregular paid "picks" that react to market events instead of a regular publishing schedule. This analysis is fact-specific and must be confirmed by a securities attorney before any commercial launch — it is general information about the law, not legal advice. If a future version ever offered personalised advice, SEC Marketing Rule 206(4)-1, Form ADV, fiduciary duty and record-keeping (5-year ad retention) would apply, and registration would turn on AUM (SEC at $110m+, otherwise the states). Separately: Securities Act §17(b) makes it unlawful to promote a security without disclosing the nature, source and amount of any compensation — the provision behind the SEC's finfluencer cases and FINRA's influencer sweep (M1 Finance $850,000; TradeZero $250,000, 2024). Anti-fraud law — Advisers Act §206, Exchange Act §10(b) / Rule 10b-5 — applies to everyone, licence or not: no disclaimer cures a false statement, a cherry-picked period, or a yield we have not verified.

3c. Market data is licensed, not scraped

Real-time U.S. equity quotes come from exchange feeds (CTA/UTP) under licence, with per-user fees — which is why free publications show ~15-minute delayed data. This concept republishes only public-domain government data (U.S. Treasury yield curves and auction results, Federal Reserve H.15, SEC EDGAR filings, IRS publications) with attribution, plus clearly dated secondary figures with their source named. It does not scrape vendor feeds or undocumented endpoints (their terms allow personal, non-commercial use only), and it does not reproduce Morningstar's, Zacks' or any other publisher's reports, methodology text or tables — facts are free to use; someone else's expression of them is not. Index names (S&P 500®, Dividend Aristocrats®) are used nominatively to identify the index; marketing a product by reference to an S&P DJI index would require a licence from S&P Dow Jones Indices. 4. Advertising & affiliate disclosure (FTC)

If this concept becomes a live publication, it may earn revenue from display advertising (e.g., Google AdSense) and affiliate relationships with brokerages, banks, and financial publishers. Under the FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255, revised 2023), a commission is a material connection that must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously — close to the recommendation and before the reader acts, not buried in a footer or behind a "more" link; the FTC's own example wording is "Paid link" or "We earn a commission if you buy through this link" ("affiliate link" alone may not be understood). A single About-page disclosure does not cover individual articles. The FTC's Consumer Reviews Rule (16 CFR Part 465, in force Oct 21, 2024) bans fake or paid-undisclosed testimonials. Sponsored placements will be labeled "Advertisement" or "Sponsor," and compensation will never buy a rating, score, or ranking. Brokerages and banks are not responsible for content on this site.
